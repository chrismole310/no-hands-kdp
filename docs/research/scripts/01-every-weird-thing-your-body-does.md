# Every Weird Thing Your Body Does, Explained

Format: catalog (Paint Explainer / Brainosophy / Stickman Scientist lane)
Target: 10:30, ~2,150 words at ~205 wpm. Ten items, escalating from "funny" to "unsettling", full-circle close.
Visual language: one stick figure ("you"), white background, ink-line props. Each item is one held drawing plus one or two inserts.

---

**[VISUAL: bedroom, stick figure in bed, eyes half closed, clock reads 11:48]**

It's 11:48 at night. You've finally stopped thinking. Your breathing slows, your jaw loosens, the room goes soft at the edges. And then, with no warning at all, your body hurls you off a cliff that isn't there.

**[VISUAL: same figure, arms flung out, one leg kicking, blanket flying]**

Your whole frame jolts. Your heart slams. You're wide awake, staring at the ceiling, and nothing happened. Nothing at all.

Your body does this to roughly seven out of ten people. It also makes you sneeze at the sun, forget why you walked into a room, and hear a stranger's voice on every recording of yourself. None of it is random. Every one of these glitches is a leftover from a much older version of you. By the end of this video you'll know what all ten of them are actually for, and the last one is the reason you can't trust your own memory.

Let's start with the cliff.

## 1. The Falling Jolt

**[VISUAL: brain drawn as a control room with two stick-figure operators, one flipping switches labelled "muscles OFF"]**

When you fall asleep, your brain shuts your muscles down in stages. It's like a building manager turning off floors one at a time. But the part of your brain that monitors your body is still half awake, and sometimes it reads that sudden relaxation the only way it knows how: as falling.

So it does what it would do if you were actually falling. It fires every muscle at once to catch you.

Sleep researchers call this a hypnic jerk. It happens more when you're exhausted, stressed, or full of caffeine, because those things make the switch-off messier. One popular idea is that this reflex is inherited from ancestors who slept in trees, where relaxing too fast really could mean falling. That part is still a guess. The part that isn't a guess is this: your brain would rather wake you up a hundred times for nothing than let you fall once.

**[VISUAL: stick figure asleep in the fork of a tree, one arm dangling]**

## 2. Goosebumps

**[VISUAL: close-up of skin as a row of hairs, each with a tiny muscle at its base, pulling upright]**

Every hair on your body has a tiny muscle attached to its root. When you're cold, or scared, or a song hits exactly right, those muscles contract and pull the hair straight up.

On a cat, that's useful. The fur puffs out, traps air, and the cat looks twice its size to whatever is threatening it.

**[VISUAL: cat puffed up like a ball next to a dog; then a stick figure with three sad little hairs standing up on its arm]**

On you, it does nothing. You lost the fur, but you kept the entire system. The muscles fire, the hairs rise, and you feel a shiver over nothing. It's the biological equivalent of a light switch in a room where the bulb was removed a million years ago.

The strange part is the music. Scientists have measured goosebumps in people listening to songs they love, and the trigger is the same fear-and-cold circuit. Your body can't tell the difference between a predator and a chorus. It just knows something big is happening.

## 3. Brain Freeze

**[VISUAL: stick figure with a milkshake, then a hand slapped to the forehead, lightning bolt over the head]**

You drink something cold too fast and ten seconds later there's a spike of pain behind your forehead. But nothing cold touched your forehead.

Here's what's actually going on. The roof of your mouth sits right underneath a bundle of blood vessels feeding your brain. Ice hits the palate, those vessels clamp down, then rush open again to warm things up. That sudden change gets picked up by the trigeminal nerve, which is the same nerve that carries feeling from your face.

**[VISUAL: cutaway of the head, a nerve drawn as a cable running from the palate up to the forehead, with a spark on it]**

The nerve can't tell where on its cable the signal started. So your brain guesses, and it guesses "forehead." The pain is real. The location is a lie.

The fix is to press your tongue flat against the roof of your mouth. You're warming the sensor.

## 4. Contagious Yawning

**[VISUAL: two stick figures on a bench; left one yawns, right one yawns half a second later]**

You've never once decided to yawn because someone else did. It just happens. And it happens more with some people than others.

In one study, researchers tracked how often people caught yawns from strangers versus friends versus family. Family came first, then friends, then acquaintances, then strangers. The closer you are to someone, the more their yawn pulls on you.

**[VISUAL: four pairs of figures with arrows of decreasing thickness: family, friends, acquaintances, strangers]**

So this isn't about being tired. It's about being connected. Yawning is one of the oldest social signals we have, older than language, and catching one is your brain quietly matching itself to the people it belongs to.

There's a second theory that yawning cools the brain, which is why you do it more when you're warm. Both could be true. Either way, if you yawned reading this, that's your brain deciding we're on the same side.

## 5. Hiccups

**[VISUAL: stick figure with the diaphragm drawn as a trampoline under the lungs, bouncing]**

A hiccup is two things happening one after the other, fast. First, the muscle under your lungs spasms and yanks in air. Then, a fraction of a second later, the flap at the top of your windpipe snaps shut. That snap is the "hic."

**[VISUAL: sequence of three frames: diaphragm drops, air arrow in, flap slams shut with the sound effect]**

It does nothing useful. It's pure reflex, and it's so pointless that scientists have looked for where it came from. One hypothesis links it to the way tadpoles breathe, gulping water in and closing the throat to keep it out of the lungs. The circuit that runs a hiccup looks a lot like that gulp. If that's right, every hiccup is a 370-million-year-old breathing program firing in a body that no longer needs it.

The reason holding your breath sometimes works: rising carbon dioxide seems to calm the circuit down. You're not stopping the hiccup. You're changing the chemistry it's listening to.

## 6. You Can't Tickle Yourself

**[VISUAL: stick figure trying to tickle its own ribs with a flat expression]**

Try it. Nothing. Someone else does the exact same thing and you fold in half.

The reason is prediction. Every time you move, a part of your brain called the cerebellum runs a forecast of what that movement will feel like, and then cancels out the feeling in advance. That's why you don't feel your own clothes all day. Your brain already knew.

**[VISUAL: brain with a small stick-figure "forecaster" holding a clipboard that says "ribs: expect tickle" and crossing it out]**

When someone else tickles you, there's no forecast. The touch arrives unannounced, and your body treats an unannounced touch on your ribs the way it would treat a spider. That laugh is a panic response with good PR.

Researchers even built a robot arm that tickled people with a delay after they pressed a button. The longer the delay, the more ticklish it got, because the forecast stopped matching.

## 7. The Doorway Effect

**[VISUAL: stick figure walking through a doorway into a kitchen, question mark over its head]**

You walk into the kitchen and stand there. You had a reason. It's gone.

This happens so reliably that psychologists gave it a name. In experiments, people carried an object across a room and were asked what they were holding. Then they carried an object through a doorway into a new room and were asked the same question. Just passing through the doorway made them forget more.

**[VISUAL: split panel: same-room walk (memory intact) vs doorway walk (memory bar drops)]**

Your brain files memories in chunks, and it uses physical boundaries to decide where one chunk ends. A doorway is a boundary. When you cross it, your brain closes the previous file and opens a new one, and whatever was on the top of the old file gets harder to reach.

It's not a defect. It's how you avoid carrying every detail of every room around all day. It just means your brain treats your hallway like a save point.

## 8. Sneezing at the Sun

**[VISUAL: stick figure stepping out of a dark building into sunlight, mid-sneeze]**

Step outside on a bright day and, for about one in four people, the sun triggers a sneeze. It has a real name, the photic sneeze reflex, and it runs in families.

The best explanation is crossed wires. The nerve that carries signals from your eyes runs close to the one that handles your nose. A sudden blast of light overloads the first, some of that signal leaks into the second, and your nose gets a message it interprets as "something's in here."

**[VISUAL: two cables running side by side, a spark jumping from the "eye" cable to the "nose" cable]**

Harmless, mostly. Unless you're a fighter pilot coming out of a cloud bank into sun, which is why the military has actually studied it.

## 9. Your Stomach Growls When You're Not Hungry

**[VISUAL: stick figure in a silent meeting, a wavy "grrrr" coming from its middle, everyone looking]**

Everyone thinks a growling stomach means hunger. Usually it doesn't.

About every ninety minutes, when your gut has been empty for a while, it runs a cleaning cycle. A wave of muscle contractions starts in your stomach and sweeps down through your intestines, pushing leftover bits along like a broom. Gas and liquid get squeezed through narrow tubes. That's the noise.

**[VISUAL: intestines drawn as a long pipe with a little stick figure pushing a broom through it]**

It's called the migrating motor complex, and it's your digestive system doing housekeeping. Eating stops it, which is why people assume the growl was hunger. It wasn't. It was a broom.

---

## 10. Your Voice Isn't Your Voice

**[VISUAL: stick figure holding a phone, listening to a recording, face of horror]**

You hear a recording of yourself and it sounds wrong. Thinner. Higher. Like a stranger doing an impression of you.

The recording is accurate. You're the one with bad information.

When you speak, sound reaches your ears two ways. One is through the air, the same way everyone else hears you. The other is through your skull. Your jaw and skull vibrate with your voice and carry it straight to your inner ear, and bone carries the low, warm frequencies best.

**[VISUAL: cutaway head, two arrows into the ear: one from the mouth through the air, one straight through the skull bone, thicker]**

So all your life you've been listening to a mix that nobody else gets. The voice you hate on the recording is the only one anyone has ever heard. Everyone who loves you loves that voice.

And that's the one that should worry you a little. Because it means the version of you that lives in your head, the one you've been checking against your whole life, was never quite the version in the room.

---

**[VISUAL: back to the bedroom, 11:48, figure drifting off again]**

So here's your body, tonight. It will try to catch you from a fall that isn't happening. It will raise fur you don't have. It will run a 370-million-year-old breathing program for no reason, cancel out the feeling of your own sheets, and file the day away in chunks you'll never fully open again.

None of it is broken. All of it is old. You are running the newest software the world has ever seen on hardware that was finished before there were words for any of this.

**[VISUAL: figure jolts once, then settles, eyes closed]**

And at some point in the next hour, it's going to throw you off that cliff again. When it does, you'll know exactly who's holding you.
