# Why You Can't Sleep Without a Blanket (Even When It's Hot)

Format: evolutionary why-we-do-it (Tp Dossier / Ink Explainer lane)
Target: 9:45, ~2,050 words at ~210 wpm. Seven-beat Ink Explainer structure: present-day scene, question, pre-empted objection, physical evidence, named researcher, artifact, full-circle close.
Visual language: one stick figure, a bed, a blanket that becomes the recurring prop. Night scenes on a dark wash.

---

**[VISUAL: bedroom at night, stick figure on the bed, sweat drops, blanket kicked to the floor]**

It's 2 a.m. in July. The room is thirty degrees. You've kicked the blanket onto the floor. You're lying there in the dark, too hot to sleep, and you are completely, absolutely free.

**[VISUAL: figure lies still for a beat, then one arm reaches down, drags the blanket back, and pulls it over the stomach]**

And you can't do it. Ten minutes later your hand goes over the edge of the bed, finds the blanket, and pulls it back over you. Not over your legs. Over your middle. One foot stays out. And somehow, hotter than you were before, you fall asleep.

You've done this your whole life and never once asked why. Why does a piece of cloth that makes you warmer help you sleep on a night when heat is the problem?

Your brain already has an answer ready: it's habit. You grew up with blankets, so you sleep with blankets. And that's part of it. But it's the smallest part. The real answer is written in your feet, in the way your brain shuts down every ninety minutes, and in a bed that was made by someone two hundred thousand years ago. By the end of this video you'll know why you do this, and you'll never look at the blanket on the floor the same way.

---

## The thermostat that has to go down

**[VISUAL: figure standing next to a big wall thermometer, red line at "37"]**

Here's the first thing nobody tells you. To fall asleep, your body has to get colder. Not your skin. Your core.

Every night, in the hour before you doze off, the temperature deep inside your chest drops by about one degree. That drop is one of the strongest signals your brain uses to open the door to sleep. If the drop doesn't happen, you lie there.

**[VISUAL: thermometer line slides from 37 down to 36, a door labelled SLEEP swings open]**

So how does your body dump heat from the inside? It sends it to the outside. Blood is pushed out to your hands and feet, where the skin is thin and packed with tiny vessels that can open wide, and the heat radiates away.

**[VISUAL: figure with hands and feet glowing red, wavy heat lines coming off them]**

Your feet are radiators. That's the job. Which is why the one-foot-out move isn't a quirk. It's the most efficient thing you do all night.

## The Swiss study with the stopwatch

**[VISUAL: a lab, a stick figure in a bed wired with little sensor dots on hands and feet, a researcher with a clipboard]**

In the late nineties a group of researchers in Basel, Switzerland wanted to know what actually predicts the moment a person falls asleep. Not what people say helps. What the body does in the minutes before it happens.

They wired volunteers up and measured everything: heart rate, core temperature, the temperature of the skin on the hands and feet. Then they waited for people to drop off and looked backward at the data.

**[VISUAL: a graph drawn on the clipboard: a "feet" line rising, a "core" line falling, the two crossing, and a big dot labelled ASLEEP]**

The single best predictor wasn't how tired someone felt. It wasn't how dark the room was. It was the gap between the temperature of their feet and the temperature of their core. When the feet warmed up and the core cooled down, sleep came fast. The bigger the gap, the faster it came.

The paper's title was five words: "Warm feet promote the rapid onset of sleep." That's it. Warm feet, fast sleep.

**[VISUAL: figure in bed, feet glowing, small "zzz"]**

Now look at what the blanket does. It doesn't warm your core. Your core is doing its own thing. It warms your skin. It builds a little pocket of air around you that sits around thirty-two, thirty-three degrees, which turns out to be almost exactly the temperature at which your hands and feet dump heat best. The blanket is not making you hotter. It's making you a better radiator.

## Every ninety minutes, your thermostat switches off

**[VISUAL: a clock face with sleep drawn as a wave, deep valleys and shallow peaks, labelled REM at the peaks]**

There's a second problem the blanket solves, and this one is stranger.

Several times a night you go into the stage of sleep where you dream. And during that stage, your body does something it never does while you're awake. It stops regulating its own temperature.

**[VISUAL: control room again, an operator asleep at the thermostat, feet up on the desk]**

You don't shiver. You don't sweat. The system that has kept you at thirty-seven degrees your entire life goes offline for ten, twenty, thirty minutes at a time. Researchers who studied this in animals found it so consistent they described dreaming sleep as a brief return to being cold-blooded. Whatever the room is doing to you in those minutes, you're just letting it.

So your body has a rule. It will only let you into that stage if the room is safe enough that it can afford to switch off. Too cold and you wake up before dreaming starts. Too hot and the same thing happens.

**[VISUAL: figure under a blanket drawn as a little tent, a thermometer inside the tent holding steady while a thermometer outside swings]**

A blanket is an external thermostat. It keeps the air around your skin steady while your internal one takes a break. And on a hot night, a light sheet over your middle does the same job as a duvet in January: it makes the air next to your skin predictable. That's what your brain is checking for. Not warmth. Predictability.

## The weight

**[VISUAL: figure in bed, a heavy blanket drawn with downward arrows, the figure's face relaxed]**

But that still doesn't explain the feeling. The relief. The way your shoulders drop the second the blanket lands on you, before it's had time to warm anything.

That part is pressure.

Steady, even pressure across the body calms the nervous system. Nobody fully understands why, but it's been measured. In 2020 a team in Sweden ran a trial with people who had chronic insomnia. Half got a blanket weighing about eight kilograms. Half got a light one. After four weeks, the heavy-blanket group was far more likely to have their insomnia drop by half. Over half of them saw that improvement. In the light group, it was under one in twenty.

**[VISUAL: two rows of stick figures, heavy-blanket row mostly asleep, light-blanket row mostly awake]**

You've felt a small version of this every night of your life. When someone puts a hand on your back. When you get a real hug. When the blanket lands. Pressure is a signal, and the signal says: something is holding you.

---

## Where the signal came from

**[VISUAL: a swaddled baby drawn as a tiny wrapped stick figure]**

Now think about the very first thing that ever happened to you.

Within minutes of being born, someone wrapped you tight in cloth. Every culture on Earth does it, and it works for the same reason: a newborn who is wrapped stops flailing and sleeps. You spent your first months learning one lesson over and over. Wrapped means safe. Wrapped means sleep. That's not a preference. That's the first association your brain ever made.

But wrapping a newborn isn't where this started either. Because people were covering themselves to sleep long before there were newborns to wrap. And we know that, because we found the bed.

---

## The oldest bed in the world

**[VISUAL: a cave mouth on a cliff, a stick figure archaeologist with a trowel]**

In South Africa, in a rock shelter called Sibudu, archaeologists were digging through layers of ancient floor. They expected tools. Fire ash. Bones.

What they found, pressed flat into a layer seventy-seven thousand years old, was a mattress.

**[VISUAL: cross-section of cave floor, a thin flattened layer of stems and leaves drawn like a sleeping mat]**

A sheet of sedges and rushes, stacked into a bed a few centimeters thick, big enough for a family. And on top of it, a layer of leaves from one specific tree. When the researchers identified the tree, they understood why. Its leaves contain chemicals that kill insects. Mosquitoes. Lice. Someone seventy-seven thousand years ago didn't just make a bed. They made a bed with built-in bug repellent, and they chose the right leaves to do it.

**[VISUAL: a stick-figure family lying on the mat, a small fire nearby, one figure pulling a hide over the others]**

A few years later, in another cave nearby, a different team found grass bedding laid over a bed of ash. The ash blocks crawling insects. That bed is two hundred thousand years old. It's older than any human art, older than any known language, close to as old as our species itself.

Now picture that night. No walls. No door. A fire, a bed of grass, animals in the dark beyond the light. What do you do with the hides you have? You pull them over you. Not for warmth alone, but because an uncovered body in the dark is a body that can be bitten, crawled on, seen. Cover is how a sleeping human stops being a target.

**[VISUAL: the hide over the family, the fire, and the outline of something four-legged at the edge of the dark]**

For two hundred thousand years, every single night, humans who covered themselves survived a little more often than humans who didn't. The ones who felt uneasy sleeping exposed left more descendants. That unease is what you feel at 2 a.m. when the blanket is on the floor. It isn't habit. It's inheritance.

---

## So here's what actually happens at 2 a.m.

**[VISUAL: back to the bedroom, the blanket on the floor, the figure lying still]**

The room is too hot. Your core is trying to dump a degree of heat and can't, because the air on your skin keeps changing. Your feet aren't radiating properly. Your brain won't let you into deep sleep because it can't switch off its thermostat in a room it doesn't trust. And somewhere far below all of that, a two-hundred-thousand-year-old alarm is quietly saying: you're uncovered, and it's dark.

**[VISUAL: the arm reaches down, the blanket comes back, one foot stays out]**

So your hand goes over the side of the bed. The blanket comes back. Not over your legs, because your feet need to radiate. Over your middle, because that's where the pressure lands and the air steadies. One foot out. And your body, which knows all of this and has never once explained it to you, finally opens the door.

**[VISUAL: figure asleep, blanket over the middle, foot out, a small fire drawn faintly in the corner of the frame]**

You'll do it again tonight. You'll do it every night for the rest of your life. And every time you do, you're doing the exact same thing someone did on a bed of grass and ash, two hundred thousand years ago, with the same hand, in the same dark, for the same reason.

They didn't have a word for it either.
