# How Long Would You Survive in Every Era of Human History?

Format: second-person survival simulation (Fossil / Paint Explainer "dying on every planet" lane)
Target: 10:45, ~2,250 words at ~210 wpm. Seven eras, one verdict each, escalating twist: the killer in every era is something nobody there could see.
Visual language: one stick figure ("you") in a T-shirt and sneakers dropped into each era. A verdict card after each era: a stick figure with a number of days, weeks, or years.

---

**[VISUAL: stick figure in T-shirt and sneakers, a swirling doorway behind it, one foot through]**

Here's the deal. You get dropped into the past. Not as a king, not as a hero, as you. Same body, same clothes, same knowledge. No phone, no medicine, no money that works. You land, the door closes, and the clock starts.

The question is simple. How long do you last?

**[VISUAL: seven doors in a row, each labelled with a date]**

We're going to do this seven times, from fifty thousand years ago to the year your grandparents were born. And there's a pattern hiding in the answers. In every single era, the thing most likely to kill you is something nobody living there could see. You'll be the only person in the world who knows it exists. Whether that saves you depends on the era.

Let's open the first door.

---

## Era 1: The Ice Age, 40,000 years ago

**[VISUAL: figure steps out into snow, wind lines, a tundra, mammoth silhouette on the horizon]**

It's Europe. The last ice age. And the first thing you notice is that your sneakers are already wet.

The cold is the obvious killer. Nights drop far below freezing and you have a cotton T-shirt. Without fire, you're in trouble by the second night. But let's say you find a band of people. They exist, they're intelligent, they're Homo sapiens exactly like you, and they have fire, hides, and forty thousand years of knowing where the food is.

**[VISUAL: a small group of fur-wrapped stick figures around a fire, looking at you with suspicion]**

Here's your problem. You can't speak to them. You can't hunt. You can't make a single tool they'd recognize. Everything you know is about a world that doesn't exist yet. To them you are a strange, soft, useless adult who has to be fed.

Whether they keep you is a coin flip. Small bands did take in strangers. They also didn't.

But suppose they do. Now the invisible killer shows up. Not a predator. A cut. You slip on ice, open your shin on a rock, and within a week it's red, hot, and swollen. There's no such thing as an antibiotic. There won't be for forty thousand years. Infections that you'd treat with a five-day course of pills killed healthy adults routinely, and they'll kill you.

**[VISUAL: verdict card: figure wrapped in a hide, "ALONE: 3 DAYS. ADOPTED: 1 TO 2 YEARS."]**

Verdict: alone, you're gone in days. Adopted, you might get a year or two before the first bad injury.

---

## Era 2: Rome, 100 AD

**[VISUAL: figure in a crowded Roman street, columns, a cart, people in tunics staring]**

Second door. Rome, at its height. A million people, running water, public baths, paved roads. This should be easy.

It isn't. You don't speak Latin. You have no papers, no family, no patron. In a city built entirely on who you belong to, you belong to no one. The most likely outcomes for a foreign adult with no connections are begging, or being taken as a slave. Neither is a death sentence. Both are a hard life.

**[VISUAL: figure holding out a hand on a street corner, a Roman walking past]**

But the thing that actually kills people in Rome isn't Romans. It's the water and the mosquitoes. The city sits next to marshes, and malaria was so common Romans had a goddess for fever. Summer in Rome killed newcomers so reliably that wealthy families left the city every year. You have no immunity. You've never had it.

And the water. The aqueducts are real and impressive, but the city's sewers empty into the same river people wash in, and stomach infections are everywhere. Dysentery, which you'd shrug off with fluids and rest, killed adults constantly.

**[VISUAL: verdict card: figure in a tunic, "6 MONTHS TO 2 YEARS"]**

Verdict: you'd probably survive the Romans. The first summer is the real test. Six months to two years.

---

## Era 3: Europe, 1348

**[VISUAL: figure in a medieval town, timber houses, a cart with a shrouded body going past]**

Third door, and this is the one you don't want. It's 1348, and the Black Death is crossing Europe.

Over the next few years it will kill somewhere between a third and a half of everyone on the continent. Not the old and the sick. Everyone. Towns lose half their people in a single summer.

**[VISUAL: a row of ten stick figures, five fade to outlines]**

You know things they don't. You know it's spread by fleas on rats, and by coughing in the pneumonic form. You know to stay out of cities, away from crowds, away from the sick. That's real knowledge and it's worth something.

But you have no immunity, no antibiotics, and nowhere to go. Every road leads to another town with the same problem. If you get the bubonic form, you have maybe a fifty percent chance. If it gets into your lungs, you have almost none, and it takes two or three days.

**[VISUAL: figure walking away from a town on an empty road, a black cloud drawn over the town behind]**

Your best move is to walk into the countryside and stay alone for two years. Some people did exactly that and lived. Most people couldn't afford to.

**[VISUAL: verdict card: figure alone on a hill, "IN A CITY: WEEKS. HIDING: YOU MIGHT MAKE IT."]**

Verdict: in a city, weeks. Alone in the hills, you have a real shot. Knowledge finally counts for something. Barely.

---

## Era 4: A ship, 1750

**[VISUAL: figure on the deck of a wooden sailing ship, ropes, a sailor climbing rigging]**

Fourth door. You wake up on a ship, somewhere in the Atlantic, in the middle of the eighteenth century.

Sailing is dangerous, but the sea isn't the invisible killer here. Food is. Specifically, one thing missing from it.

After about six to eight weeks without fresh fruit or vegetables, your body runs out of vitamin C. Your gums swell and bleed. Old wounds reopen. Your teeth loosen. Then you die. This is scurvy, and on long voyages it killed more sailors than storms, battles, and shipwrecks combined.

**[VISUAL: figure at a ship's table with hardtack and salt meat, a calendar flipping past week six, the figure's gums drawn bleeding]**

Here's the twist. In 1747, a Scottish naval surgeon named James Lind took twelve sailors with scurvy, split them into pairs, and gave each pair a different treatment. Cider. Vinegar. Sea water. Oranges and lemons. The pair on oranges and lemons were back on their feet in a week. He had proven the cure. It would take the British navy nearly fifty more years to act on it.

**[VISUAL: six pairs of sick figures, one pair standing up healthy holding oranges]**

You know the cure. So does one doctor. The question is whether anyone on your ship has citrus, and whether the captain listens to a stranger in sneakers.

**[VISUAL: verdict card: figure holding a lemon, "WITH CITRUS: THE WHOLE VOYAGE. WITHOUT: 3 MONTHS."]**

Verdict: if you can get your hands on fruit, you survive the voyage. If you can't, three months.

---

## Era 5: London, 1854

**[VISUAL: figure on a foggy London street, gas lamp, a water pump on the corner]**

Fifth door. Victorian London. The biggest city on Earth. Factories, trains, newspapers.

And cholera. That summer, in one neighborhood, hundreds of people died in ten days. The disease empties you out so fast that a healthy adult can be dead in twelve hours. Everyone believed it was carried by bad air. Bad smells. Fog.

**[VISUAL: a doctor stick figure pointing at a cloud of "miasma" while a person drinks from the pump behind him]**

One physician didn't believe it. John Snow walked the streets of Soho and marked every death on a map. The deaths clustered around a single water pump on Broad Street. He talked the local council into removing the pump's handle. The outbreak collapsed.

**[VISUAL: a street map, black dots clustering around one pump, then a hand removing the handle]**

You'd know this the moment you arrived. Don't drink the water. Boil it. Stay away from the pump.

And that changes everything. For the first time in this video, your knowledge is worth more than the knowledge of everyone around you. You're a stranger with no money in a brutal city, and you can find work, because the city is starving for workers. You'll be poor. You'll be filthy. But you'll be drinking boiled water and washing your hands, and that alone puts you ahead of almost every person in London.

**[VISUAL: verdict card: figure holding a kettle, "YEARS. MAYBE DECADES."]**

Verdict: years. If you avoid the factories and the water, maybe decades.

---

## Era 6: 1918

**[VISUAL: figure on a city street, people in cloth masks, a streetcar]**

Sixth door. Autumn, 1918. The war is ending. And a flu is starting.

It will kill somewhere around fifty million people worldwide in about two years, more than the war did. And here's what makes it different from every other killer in this video: it goes after the young and healthy. Twenty to forty year olds died at rates nobody had seen before. Your strong immune system isn't protection. In this flu, it might be the problem.

**[VISUAL: a bar chart in stick figures, the tallest bar labelled "20–40"]**

You know exactly what this is. You know it spreads through the air. You know masks help, distance helps, and that the cities that shut down early did better than the cities that held parades. Philadelphia held a parade. Two weeks later its hospitals were full.

**[VISUAL: two cities side by side, one with a parade and a rising line, one with closed doors and a flat line]**

So you stay home. You wear a mask. You avoid crowds for eighteen months. It's boring and it's lonely and it's the correct answer. But there's no vaccine, and if it finds you anyway, the medicine of 1918 has almost nothing to offer.

**[VISUAL: verdict card: figure in a mask, "A COIN FLIP FOR 2 YEARS. THEN, A LIFE."]**

Verdict: get through the two years, and you've got a normal life ahead of you. The two years are a coin flip.

---

## Era 7: 1950

**[VISUAL: figure on a suburban street, a car with fins, a milk bottle on a step]**

Last door. 1950. And the first thing you feel is relief.

Penicillin has existed for a decade and is being made by the ton. The cut on your shin from the Ice Age is a two-dollar problem now. Vaccines for the worst childhood diseases are arriving. The water is treated. The food is inspected.

**[VISUAL: figure holding a small bottle of pills, a big green tick]**

You'd still have problems. No records, no birth certificate, no way to explain where you came from. And smoking is everywhere, and nobody knows what it does yet. That's the invisible killer of 1950: it's in the air of every room, and it will take thirty years to show up.

But you know. So you don't.

**[VISUAL: verdict card: figure, older, "A FULL LIFE."]**

Verdict: you live. Most of the way through.

---

## The pattern

**[VISUAL: the seven verdict cards in a row, the survival number rising from left to right]**

Look at the seven answers together. Days. Months. Weeks. Months. Years. A coin flip. A life.

Now look at what killed you in each one. Not the cold. Not the Romans. Not the storms or the war. A bacterium in a cut. A parasite in a mosquito. A germ on a flea. A missing vitamin. A germ in a pump. A virus in the air. Smoke.

**[VISUAL: seven tiny drawings, each of the invisible killer, in a row]**

In every era, the thing most likely to kill you was invisible. And in every era, the people living there were fighting something else. The Ice Age band was watching for wolves. Rome was building walls. Medieval Europe was praying. Sailors were bracing for storms. Londoners were holding their noses against the fog.

They weren't stupid. They were fighting what they could see.

**[VISUAL: figure standing alone facing the seven doors, holding a small drawing of a germ]**

You'd walk into any of those eras as the only person alive who knew what was actually there. Not stronger. Not braver. Just able to see one more thing. And the eras where that let you live are the eras where someone finally listened. A surgeon with twelve sailors. A doctor with a map. A city that closed its doors.

**[VISUAL: the doors fade, the figure is back in the present, phone in hand]**

Which leaves one question. Right now, in your era, what's the invisible thing? What are you fighting that you can see, while the real one sits quietly in the room?

You'll find out in about fifty years. Someone will make a video about it.
